Reproduction & hardware deployment

Passwd‑ABC: the paper's figures, reproduced — and put on silicon

A figure-by-figure comparison of our reproduction against Badea & Montejo Berlingen, “A data-driven and model-agnostic approach to combinatorial assignment problems” (arXiv:2309.05728) — then one step the paper never took: running the anomaly detector on a real FPGA.

Orientation

What’s being compared

The paper introduces Passwd‑ABC, an autoencoder-based network trained only on QCD background that assigns jets to parent particles and emits an anomaly score. It reports three data panels: the average reconstructed mass and the reconstruction loss (Fig. 2), and a per-layer / heuristic mass comparison (Fig. 3). Figure 1 is an architecture schematic and carries no data, so it is not reproduced here.

Every “ours” panel below is produced with the paper’s own released weights (config_files/2309.05728) run on our simulated samples — a like-for-like check of the published model. The final section is different: a retrained, hardware-synthesizable variant executing on a Xilinx VCK190.

Structure reproduced

Background piles up at HT/2 ≈ 500 GeV exactly as in the paper, while every signal is pushed out to its mass scale — the model reconstructs pair-produced particles it never saw in training. The one difference is a mild downward bias of ~100–200 GeV in our peaks (the released weights reconstruct the 1500 GeV points near 1300 and the 2000 GeV points near 1400–1700).

Figure 2 · bottom

Reconstruction loss — the anomaly score

Discrimination reproduced

Background sits at low loss; signals shift high. The paper quotes background rejection of O(200) at 50% signal efficiency (equivalent to AUC ≈ 0.98–0.99). Our released-weights reproduction lands at AUC 0.89–0.99 across the five signals — the two 2000 GeV points and the high-multiplicity 10j reach the paper’s regime; the lowest sit just under it.

Figure 3

Last layer vs. first layer vs. minimum-asymmetry

Full narrative reproduced

All three of the paper’s points come through on the example signal (stop 12j, green): the last layer is the sharpest reconstruction; the first ABC layer is broader and biased high, so the second-layer refinement demonstrably improves resolution; and the minimum-mass-asymmetry heuristic is broadest of all, with a high-mass tail that sculpts the background upward — the paper’s argument for why the learned metric beats the heuristic.

Beyond the paper

The anomaly detector, running on an FPGA

The paper is entirely in software. We took the model to a Xilinx VCK190. That required first fixing the deployed model — the port had collapsed (every jet assigned to the ISR category, a flat loss, AUC 0.35) because the training loss was missing the paper’s anti-collapse term. After retraining a non-collapsed variant and rebuilding the bitstream, the reconstruction-loss panel measured on real silicon separates signal from background just as cleanly as software:

0.967
VCK190 hardware AUC
software 0.96  ·  cycle-accurate sim 0.96  ·  collapsed port 0.35
Two bugs found along the way

Reproducing the paper surfaced a training bug (the missing triplet/anti-collapse loss let the model collapse to ISR) and a sample-generation bug (a zero-width, stable neutralino carried half of three signals away as invisible energy, flattening their mass to the background’s). Fixed, both the software reproduction and the hardware model recover the paper’s discrimination.

Bottom line

Where it matches, and where it doesn’t

Matches the paper: background at 500 GeV; signals separated in both mass and loss; the loss correlated with reconstructed mass; the first→last layer resolution improvement; and the min-asymmetry heuristic sculpting the background — every qualitative claim in the data figures.

Differs from the paper: our released-weights reconstruction carries a mild ~100–200 GeV downward mass bias, so the lowest-mass points reach AUC ~0.90 rather than the headline O(200) rejection; and the absolute loss scale differs by an offset (only the relative separation is comparable). Neither changes a physics conclusion.

Goes past the paper: a retrained, quantized variant runs the whole pipeline on FPGA fabric and reproduces the anomaly separation on real hardware (AUC 0.967) — something the paper does not attempt.
